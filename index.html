About Me

Hi! I'm Jen. Welcome to my page and thanks for stopping by! I am an astrophysicist at Stanford University by day, but by night (and on weekends) I love to garden and read. I completed my Ph.D. at Columbia University where I was an NSF Graduate Research Fellow and serial "yes" person. During my Ph.D., I had a really tough time chosing between my background in computational astrophysics and the rich observational datasets...so I didn't chose! I also didn't choose between my research, teaching, and outreach which made for a busy, but fun(!) graduate experience. My primary research interest is in chemical evolution and galaxy formation and evolution spanning from the formation of the very first stars just 300 million years after the Big Bang until today. I consider myself a computational astrophysicist, a galactic archaeologist, a teacher, and a communicator, all in one. You can check out some of my research, teaching materials, and outreach materials on my page. And as always, I'd love to hear from you if you have ideas for collaborations or just want to connect!

Research

The study of chemical evolution is, in many ways, the study of the evolution of the Universe itself. Stars sit at the center of it all, acting both as cosmic alchemists, producing elements heavier than H and He that go on to enrich further star formation, and as cosmic record-keepers, recording in their surface abundances the history of star formation that came before. My research thus spans cosmic time, from the first stars to the Milky Way, exploring how chemical evolution kicks off in the early Universe, the origin of the elements, and the signatures left behind by the first stars and subsequent generations in stars that are observable in the Milky Way and Local Group. Addressing questions at each end of cosmic time requires an approach that applies astrophysical theory to (presently) unobserved epochs of star formation (computational astrophysics) and connect with the stars observable in our own backyard (galactic archaeology).

Computational and Analytical Astrophysics

The first stars (Population III; Pop III) have yet to be observed 1 during the epoch during which Pop III was the dominant mode of star formation. Pop III stars form from gas unenriched by elements heavier than He, making the physics of Pop III formation unique compared to the formation of all other subsequent star formation. Namely, it is expected that Pop III stars are more massive than their younger Pop II (enriched) cousins, meaning that most if not all Pop III stars had short lifespans, whereas even the generation of Pop II stars that immediately followed Pop III may be observable in our own Milky Way. Numerical and analytical models of early galaxy formation enable studies of the relevant physics and predictions of observables that connect to the regime observed by JWST as well as the metal-poor ancient stars that survive in the Milky Way halo and dwarf galaxies.

Pop III stars form by the handful, only a few per galaxy before their feedback enriches the surrounding gas, triggering a transition to Pop II formation. Because of the stochasticity of Pop III formation, the properties of each feedback event as well as spatial and temporal placement play a critical role in the early chemical evolution of galaxies. Thus, while many traditional numerical simulations model stars as populations averaged over the initial mass function, this assumption is insufficient when modeling Pop III and a star-by-star approach is required. The AEOS simulations are a set of cosmological simulations that employ such a star-by-star model for both Pop III and Pop II stars, enabling detailed studies of the formation of Pop III, and the transition from Pop III to Pop II.

1 The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has observed several "Pop III candidate" galaxies.

Teaching

The most important skill a science course can teach is how to think like a scientist: how to ask a well-defined question, weigh evidence, take uncertainty seriously, and explain your reasoning to others. Curiosity sits at the center of my classroom, because the value of learning lies as much in the exploring as in the answers, and students should leave confident in their ability to ask questions and find their own answers.

Intentional Engagement as a Scientist

Science is not a collection of facts; it is a way of deciding what to believe based on available evidence and the flexibility to modify your view of the world. The most important lesson I can offer a student in a STEM classroom is not a particular result about stars or galaxies, but the habit of asking how do we know? and the confidence to answer that question for themselves. Evidence-based reasoning should not be an implicit byproduct of STEM training, something students are expected to absorb by watching experts. It should be taught deliberately, practiced routinely, and assessed directly. My teaching is built around making that reasoning visible, and around widening the ways students can engage with science so that more of them come to see it as something they can do.

Evidence-Based Reasoning as Standard Practice

Students often arrive in introductory science courses believing that to succeed, they only need to produce the right answer. When that is the only result rewarded, students learn to match patterns rather than to reason, and they leave without the tools to apply their knowledge beyond the classroom. Ultimately, this as a structural problem, and so I address it structurally: in how I design activities, in what I ask students to write down, and in what I choose to grade.

When I designed the laboratory curriculum for Astronomy Lab II at Columbia University (ASTR UN1904), I organized each lab around the flow of real scientific inquiry: making a prediction from a physical model, confronting that prediction with data, and deciding what the comparison tells us. Rather than presenting conclusions such as the existence of dark matter as settled facts to memorize, the labs ask students to study the evidence themselves and then make their own conclusion about what the evidence suggests. Students should experience a scientific conclusion as something flexible and derived through evidence, not as something settled and handed to them.

What we grade tells students what we value. When grading, I weight the clarity and justification of reasoning alongside correctness. What they did, how they did it, and why matters as much as how they communicate their process and the conclusion they reached. Importantly, I also encourage students to acknowledge when an answer they derive is unreasonable and propose a hypothesis for why. Noticing that something has gone wrong, and reasoning about the source of the error, is one of the most important skills a scientist can have, and I want students to feel that it is valued rather than penalized.

Feedback as a Conversation

Learning happens through feedback, and in the classroom, feedback has to flow in both directions. Students need clear, timely guidance on their thinking, and instructors need honest insight into how their teaching is landing.

As an instructor, I commit to detailed feedback that identifies where not only where they may have gone wrong, but where they went right to reinforce good practices. When providing feedback on work that reached the wrong or flawed conclusions, I aim to identify and make clear where the error in reasoning occurred so students can retrace their steps and learn from them. Paired with a grading scheme that rewards justification and self-correction, this approach treats mistakes as part of the learning process rather than something to hide.

Feedback from students is just as important to me. Each lab closes with two questions: what questions students still have, and what they thought of the lab itself. What was clear, what was confusing, or what they liked or disliked. Asking about remaining questions signals that science does not end when the worksheet does, and that uncertainty is a normal part of understanding. It also shows me where a concept needs more attention, which I carve out time to address at the beginning of the following class. Asking directly for feedback on the lab tells students that their experience matters and that the course is something we shape together. I use these responses to adjust how I explain ideas and structure activities, so that students can see their input reflected in the classroom.

Expanding Modes of Engagement

Students arrive in the classroom through many different doors, figuratively and literally. One of the ways a student most quickly comes to believe "science is not for me" is being unable to engage with the material or demonstrate their understanding in a way that suites their learning style and background. Broadening the ways students can engage is both a matter of equity and of good pedagogy: when students can bring their own interests and strengths into the room, they invest more deeply and reason more carefully.

This principle runs through the structure of my labs, my classroom, and my assessments. Students move between quantitative derivation, hand-drawn and digital plotting, interactive simulations, reading about the people behind major discoveries, and conversations with practicing researchers. Students choose whether to keep a physical or electronic notebook, whether to work with a partner or independently, which tools they use to analyze data, and how, where, and to whom they ask questions. These choices are small individually, but together they communicate that there is more than one legitimate way to do science well.

Learning should also extend beyond the classroom and the boundaries of the syllabus. To support this, I offer an online platform where students can engage with myself and each other about astronomy. Students are encouraged to answer one another’s questions, share articles and discoveries they came across, and follow threads that interested them. Importantly, this gives quieter students a low-pressure way to participate and builds a sense of community so that students who feel connected to their peers are more willing to ask questions, admit confusion, and take intellectual risks.

The final project is where this philosophy is most fully expressed. Students choose an astronomy topic that genuinely intrigues them, explore it in depth using real sources, and then communicate what they learned through a creative medium of their choice. What matters is that a student convey ideas clearly and compellingly. This freedom turns the project from a task into a way for students to bring their own backgrounds and strengths into a new field. It also reflects something about science itself: knowledge only matters if it can be shared, and the ability to communicate science to different audiences, in different forms, is a professional skill that is rarely taught.

Looking Forward

In every course I teach, I aim to make the process of scientific reasoning explicit, to assess what I claim to value, and to open as many paths to access the material as I can. My students should leave able to weigh evidence, to be comfortable with uncertainty, and to be confident that their curiosity and ways of thinking have a place in science. As I continue to teach, I look forward to refining these practices, drawing on the growing body of research on how students learn, and helping to make intentional training in evidence-based reasoning a standard expectation across STEM education.
